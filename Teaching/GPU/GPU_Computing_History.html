How We Got Here

From fixed-function triangle rasterizers to tensor cores, the story of how an industry built to serve gamers accidentally created the most important computing platform of the twenty-first century.

Section 1 – The pixel problem Top ↑

A 1080p frame is two million pixels.  At 60 frames per second, that is 120 million color decisions per second.  The triangle rasterization method makes each of those decisions independent of every other – and that independence is what made the problem solvable with fixed-function hardware: silicon that could do exactly one thing – rasterize triangles – and nothing else.

The hundred-thousand-dollar workstation

Before GPUs existed, real-time 3D graphics belonged to Silicon Graphics.  SGI sold workstations that cost more than houses – $100,000 and up – to Hollywood studios, the military, and aerospace companies.  Jurassic Park's dinosaurs, flight simulators for the Air Force, crash simulations for automakers – that was where real-time 3D rendering lived.  The entire market was professional, the budgets were enormous, and the idea that a $200 consumer card would eventually obliterate this business model was, in 1992, absurd.

SGI's hardware used custom graphics pipelines built from specialized chips.  The RealityEngine (1993) could render more than a million lit, textured, antialiased polygons per second – the design target had been half that, and the shipped hardware doubled it.  A staggering number at the time, achieved with dedicated rasterization hardware that did one job and did it fast.  This was not general-purpose computing.  These chips could not run a spreadsheet.  They were hardwired to transform vertices, clip polygons to the screen boundary, interpolate colors across triangle surfaces, and write pixel values to a frame buffer.  Every step was fixed in silicon.

The fixed-function approach worked because the graphics pipeline is inherently sequential in structure but massively parallel in data.  Every triangle passes through the same stages – vertex transformation, clipping, rasterization, texturing, depth test – but millions of triangles can be in flight simultaneously, each independent of the others.  If you know exactly what operations will be performed, you can build a circuit that performs them without the overhead of instruction fetch, decode, and scheduling that a general-purpose processor requires.  You trade flexibility for throughput.  The pendulum, at this moment, is fully on the special-purpose side.

Jim Clark, SGI's founder, left the company in 1994 to start Netscape.  Even the man who built the graphics workstation industry thought the web was a bigger bet than graphics hardware.  He was right about the web.  He was wrong about graphics – but the future of graphics would not belong to SGI.  It would belong to the consumer market that SGI never took seriously.

Why triangles

Every surface you have ever seen rendered on a computer – every game character, every CAD model, every animated film – was built from triangles. Not squares, not arbitrary polygons, not curves.  Triangles.  This is not an accident of history or a limitation of early hardware.  It is a mathematical necessity that was recognized long before computers existed – and then put to work, with consequences nobody anticipated, by an engineer at a Boeing facility in Seattle.

In 1980, Loren Carpenter was working in Boeing's computer-aided design group, trying to render realistic terrain around airplane models.  He wanted mountains.  The standard approach – hand-modeling each peak and valley with control points – was too slow and produced landscapes that looked artificial.  Then, browsing a bookshop on University Avenue in Seattle, he found Benoît Mandelbrot's Fractals: Form, Chance and Dimension – and read it cover to cover, twice.  Fractals.  Self-similar geometry at every scale.

“I knew at once that I could apply that math to my problem,” Carpenter later recalled.  His technique was midpoint subdivision: take a triangle, displace the midpoints of its edges by a random amount, and split it into four smaller triangles.  Repeat.  At each level of recursion, the surface gains detail – ridges, valleys, the jagged fractal structure of real terrain.  The mathematics of this decomposition – breaking surfaces into simplices, the simplest possible polygonal element – had been developed by Henri Poincaré in the 1890s for studying topology.  Poincaré was not thinking about rendering.  He was classifying surfaces.  But the tool he built turned out to be exactly what Carpenter needed, eight decades later.

On July 14 of that year, Carpenter presented Vol Libre at SIGGRAPH – a two-minute film of a camera soaring over fractally generated mountains, banking and diving like a hang glider, finally coming to rest on a tiny Utah teapot sitting on a mountainside.  The audience of a thousand engineers gave him a standing ovation.  Ed Catmull and Alvy Ray Smith – already legends in the field, running the computer graphics division at Lucasfilm – walked up afterward and asked if he could start in October.  A Boeing CAD engineer, hired on the spot, because he saw what a triangle could do.

Carpenter died on December 21, 2025, at the age of 78.  His technique – listed by IEEE Spectrum as “the midpoint subdivision method for rendering fractals” – became the Genesis effect in Star Trek II: The Wrath of Khan, the first fully computer-generated sequence in a feature film.  He went on to co-invent the Reyes rendering architecture and co-author PhotoRealistic RenderMan at Pixar.  But the essential insight happened in a bookshop, recognizing century-old mathematics when he saw it.

The rasterization bargain

The reason triangles won – and the reason GPU hardware is built around them to this day – is a bargain between mathematics and silicon.  A triangle is the 2D simplex: the minimum polygon.  Three vertices define a plane, always, with no ambiguity.  A quad can be non-planar.  A pentagon can be concave.  A triangle cannot.  This guarantee means you can build a circuit that answers “is this pixel inside this triangle?” with three edge-function evaluations – a handful of multiplies and compares per pixel.  Degenerate triangles still exist, but they are local cases the rasterizer can reject or handle with fixed rules.

In 1988, Juan Pineda published “A Parallel Algorithm for Polygon Rasterization” at SIGGRAPH, formalizing the edge-function approach that modern GPU rasterizers still use.  The algorithm is inherently parallel: every pixel can be tested independently.  You can evaluate thousands of edge functions simultaneously if you have enough hardware, and the result is always correct because the triangle’s mathematical properties guarantee it.  Gary Tarolli, the CTO of 3dfx, designed the Voodoo rasterizer using Pineda-style setup and rasterization.  Every GPU since has used some descendant of the same idea.

This is the rasterization bargain: you accept that every surface in the world will be approximated as a mesh of flat triangles, and in return you get hardware that can process those triangles at extraordinary speed because the per-triangle computation is small, uniform, and embarrassingly parallel.  The approximation is lossy – a sphere is not really made of flat facets – but if you use enough triangles, the human eye cannot tell.  And the number of triangles you can afford is exactly the question that drives GPU hardware scaling.

The fixed-function graphics pipeline of the 1990s was the purest expression of this bargain.  Every transistor on the chip was dedicated to one task: turning a stream of triangles into a frame of pixels, as fast as possible.  The pipeline could not do anything else.  It could not sort a list, multiply matrices for reasons unrelated to graphics, or run a neural network.  It was a machine for rasterizing triangles, and that is all it was.

That was about to change – not because anyone wanted general-purpose computing on a graphics chip, but because game developers wanted prettier water.

Section 2 – The game engine that ate everything Top ↑

In 1996 a small company called 3dfx shipped the first 3D accelerator a regular person could afford: a card called the Voodoo Graphics, which slotted into a home PC and did exactly one job, draw 3D for games.  It cost around $300 and could not draw a single 2D window.  You kept your existing 2D card and let the Voodoo take over the instant a game went full-screen.  By every measure of versatility it was a worse machine than the workstation SGI sold for a hundred times the price.  And it won.  The question this section answers is why the worse machine was the one that kept getting better – and the answer runs as much through the companies that bet on it as through the silicon itself.

The answer is the arithmetic of volume.  A workstation sold in the thousands at six figures each; a consumer 3D card sold in the millions at a few hundred dollars.  The fixed cost of designing a chip – the mask sets, the verification, the tooling – is enormous and barely changes whether you ship ten thousand units or ten million.  Spread across millions of buyers it nearly vanishes per unit, and what is left over funds the next chip.  The mass market turned out to be the only market that could afford to keep paying for better silicon, generation after generation.  This is the economic engine that funds everything later in this series – including the hardware that now trains neural networks.

Workstation techniques, mass-market price

The consumer 3D industry was built, in large part, by engineers who came out of the high-end graphics-workstation world of the previous section – the expensive machines built specifically to render 3D.  In 1994 three Silicon Graphics engineers – Ross Smith, Scott Sellers, and Gary Tarolli – left to found 3dfx Interactive; Tarolli is the same engineer whose Pineda-style rasterizer closed the previous section.  They took the techniques that made those graphics workstations expensive and put them on a card a teenager could buy with a summer job.  What the Voodoo delivered was smooth, textured, perspective-correct 3D at frame rates that made software rendering look like a slideshow.  The first time a player saw it run, they wanted one.  Quake, in particular, sold cards: people bought a Voodoo to play it, and for a generation that was the moment 3D stopped being a novelty.  The math inside it was not new – it was the rasterization and interpolation of the graphics-workstation era.  What was new was the price, and therefore the volume, and therefore the budget for the next chip.  The expensive ideas had found a cheaper home.

Why open standards beat fast ones

A graphics API is the contract between a game and the hardware: the game describes triangles and textures, and the API hands them to whatever chip is installed.  3dfx wrote its own contract – a proprietary interface called Glide, thin and fast because it mapped almost directly onto Voodoo silicon.  For a few years that was a genuine edge: around a hundred and fifty games supported Glide.  But proprietary cuts both ways.  A game written for Glide ran only on 3dfx cards; a game written for Microsoft’s Direct3D or for OpenGL ran on every card from every vendor.  Once the open standards caught up on quality, that portability decided everything: the sensible target was the API that reached every card, not the one that reached only 3dfx.  Fast-but-proprietary lost to good-enough-and-everywhere.

A self-inflicted wound finished the proprietary bet.  For most of its life 3dfx sold chips to board makers – companies like Creative and Diamond – who designed, built, and sold the actual cards.  In 1998 it bought one of them, STB Systems, and began making its own cards.  In one move it turned its customers into competitors, and they moved their business to the chip supplier that did not compete with them.  By the end of 2000 the company that had introduced consumer 3D had stopped making cards and sold its remaining technology – mostly patents – to NVIDIA.  The lesson that outlives the names is the one about the contract: the hardware that won was the hardware anyone could write for.

Betting against the grain of the hardware

NVIDIA, the company that bought those patents, had come close to dying first.  It was founded in 1993 – three engineers, Jensen Huang, Chris Malachowsky, and Curtis Priem, sketching a 3D-chip company in a booth at a Denny’s in East San Jose, naming it after invidia, the Latin word for envy.  Their first chip, the NV1 (1995), made a confident bet against the lesson of the previous section: instead of decomposing everything into triangles, it rendered curved surfaces directly, as quadratic patches.  It was a real idea with a real advantage – curved shapes need fewer primitives than a triangle mesh – but it ran against the grain of the hardware.  The previous section showed why triangles are the parallel-friendly primitive: each pixel asks one independent question, in any order, with no shared state.  Quadratic patches give that up.  So when Direct3D standardized on triangles, and only triangles, the quadratic approach was not merely off-standard – it was off-hardware, betting against the very structure that makes a GPU fast.

The bet nearly ended the company.  NVIDIA spent a year building a quadratic chip for Sega’s Dreamcast; Sega cancelled it and switched vendors, but kept NVIDIA alive with a five-million-dollar investment.  Half the staff was laid off, and when the RIVA 128 shipped in 1997 the company had about one month of payroll left in the bank.  The RIVA 128 used triangles, aligned with Direct3D, and sold.  NVIDIA had course-corrected onto the standard, and it never bet against it again.

The pendulum returns

By the end of the decade the consumer GPU had absorbed almost the entire rendering pipeline into dedicated hardware.  The last large piece moved in 1999, when NVIDIA’s GeForce 256 pulled the geometry stage off the CPU and onto the card.  That stage transforms each vertex from model space into screen space and computes how light falls on it.  NVIDIA marketed it as the world’s first GPU, a term it defined as a single chip that did transform, lighting, triangle setup, and rendering on its own, at a minimum of ten million polygons per second; the “GeForce” name was short for Geometry Force, and whether it was truly the first has been argued over ever since.  But notice the direction of travel.  The previous section ended with the pipeline fixed in silicon; this one ends by fixing one more stage, hardware transform and lighting, into that silicon.  The chip was not becoming more general.  It was swinging further toward special-purpose, absorbing more of a known, unchanging pipeline into dedicated circuits – the same bargain as rasterization, one stage earlier.  NVIDIA and ATI, whose Radeon line arrived in 2000 and which AMD would absorb in 2006, were the two that came out the far side of the GPU wars; everyone else was acquired or gone.

The gaming market had produced a chip that did every step of 3D rendering in hardware, faster every year, funded by an audience of millions who only wanted better games.  For a while that looked like the end of the story: a fully fixed-function machine, perfected.  But fixed-function has a built-in limit – it can only produce the effects its designers anticipated.  The next pressure on the GPU did not come from a company or a standards committee.  It came from developers who wanted light, water, and surfaces to behave in ways no fixed pipeline could express, and who would not take no for an answer.  To give them that, the hardware would have to become, for the first time, programmable.  That is the crack in the wall.

Section 3 – The crack in the wall Top ↑

A fixed-function pipeline does a fixed list of things.  The geometry stage transformed and lit each vertex with the one lighting model built into it; the stages after it combined a few textures in a small set of preset ways.  For most of a decade that list was enough, because it covered what most games asked for.  But a developer who wanted a surface the list did not cover – skin that scatters light softly, water that bends what is behind it, metal that streaks the way real metal does – had only two choices: fake it with clever combinations of the presets, or wait for the next chip to add the effect to the list.  A fixed pipeline can only produce the effects its designers thought of in advance, and by 2000 the people making games had run past that edge.

What a shader is

The answer arrived in two steps.  In November 2000 Microsoft shipped DirectX 8, and in February 2001 NVIDIA shipped the GeForce 3 to run it.  Together they introduced something the pipeline had never allowed: instead of choosing from a menu of built-in operations, a developer could write a small program that the GPU would execute as part of drawing.  Such a program is called a shader.  There were two kinds.  A vertex shader ran once for every vertex and decided where that vertex landed and what it carried; a pixel shader ran once for every pixel a triangle covered and decided that pixel’s final color.  Microsoft named the arrangement Shader Model 1.0.

Set those two jobs against the earlier sections and the change is striking.  The vertex shader is the transform-and-lighting stage from the previous section – except the developer now writes the math rather than accepting the single model baked into the GeForce 256.  The pixel shader is the per-pixel function from the first section – the question asked independently of every other pixel – except it, too, is now something a developer writes.  The fixed-function unit had become a tiny programmable processor.  Each shader was a handful of instructions running on a small arithmetic engine – an ALU, the part of any processor that does the actual adds and multiplies – and the GPU ran that same little program across millions of vertices or pixels at once.

The first shaders were humble: written in an assembly-like notation, capped at a few dozen instructions, with no loops and no branches – closer to a pocket calculator than to a programming language.  Even so, the effect was immediate, because the developer now defined the lighting math instead of selecting it from a list.  Early titles such as Morrowind and Splinter Cell used the new per-pixel control for lighting and shadow the fixed pipeline could not have produced; Unreal Engine 2 soon made shaders ordinary; and the first Xbox, built around a GeForce 3 derivative, put the same capability under millions of televisions.

From calculator to processor

Then the programs grew up.  Each generation made the shader less like a calculator and more like a real processor.  Shader Model 2.0, arriving with DirectX 9 in 2002 on cards like ATI’s Radeon 9700, added floating-point arithmetic and far longer programs.  Shader Model 3.0, on NVIDIA’s GeForce 6 series in 2004, added the things that make code actually code: branches, so a shader could choose different work depending on its input, and loops, so it could repeat work a variable number of times.  With each step the shader could express more on its own and lean less on the fixed scaffolding around it.

The pendulum reverses

This was the pendulum swinging the other way.  The previous section ended with the GPU at its most special-purpose, every stage frozen in silicon.  Programmable shaders pried one stage, and then another, back open – the first real movement in twenty years toward a more general machine.  But the motive was still entirely about graphics: shaders existed to make better-looking games, and they spoke only in vertices, pixels, and textures.  What almost no one set out to build was the implication sitting in plain view.  A pixel shader is a small program that runs the same arithmetic across millions of independent elements at once.  Forget what the elements are – forget that they are pixels – and that is simply a parallel computer.  Someone was going to notice, and what they did about it became one of the stranger chapters in the story of the hardware.

Section 4 – The era of the hack Top ↑

The previous section ended on a quiet implication: a pixel shader is a small program that runs the same arithmetic across millions of independent elements at once, and nothing about it requires those elements to be pixels.  Around 2003 a handful of researchers began taking that seriously.  The GPU was by then the fastest parallel arithmetic engine an ordinary person could buy – far quicker at bulk math than any CPU of the day – so the question was whether it could be made to do arithmetic that had nothing to do with graphics.  It could.  But only by lying to it.

Computation disguised as drawing

The trick was to dress an ordinary computation up as a rendering job.  To the GPU a texture is just a grid of numbers it knows how to read, so a programmer would pack the input – an array of values, the cells of a simulation, the entries of a matrix – into a texture, treating each texel’s color channels as plain numbers rather than colors.  The computation itself was written as a pixel shader.  To run it, the programmer drew a single rectangle the size of the desired output, which forced the GPU to fire the shader once for every output element, all in parallel.  The shader read from the input textures, did its arithmetic, and wrote its answer as a “color” into an output texture, which was then read back as data.  To add two arrays of a million numbers, in other words, you drew a rectangle.

Stated plainly it sounds absurd, and it was – but it worked, and it was fast.  Through the middle of the decade researchers folded one problem after another into the shape of a drawing operation: fluid and physics simulations, the linear-algebra routines underneath most scientific computing, the Fast Fourier Transform, even early work on options pricing and protein folding.  Each was expressed as a sequence of rectangles drawn into textures.  The practice acquired a name – GPGPU, for general-purpose computation on the GPU – and a small, inventive community formed around the craft of making graphics hardware do work that had nothing to do with graphics.

BrookGPU

The contortions were the obvious thing to attack next.  Even with a higher-level shading language, a GPGPU program was not written as computation; it was written as a run of graphics calls – pack the data into textures by hand, load and bind the shader, render the geometry that invokes it.  In 2004 a Stanford group led by the graduate student Ian Buck, with his advisor Pat Hanrahan and several others, presented Brook for GPUs: a language that let a programmer write the computation directly.  Brook extended ordinary C with two ideas – a stream, which is simply a collection of data to be processed in parallel, and a kernel, a function applied to every element of a stream – and its compiler quietly turned the streams into textures and the kernels into shaders underneath.  The graphics API was still down there doing the work; Brook only hid it, so the programmer could think about the computation instead of the disguise.

The limits of the costume

For all its ingenuity, the era was built on a workaround, and the seams showed.  A shader could read from anywhere in its input textures but could write only to its own single output location: it could gather freely yet could not scatter, so any algorithm that needed to write results to arbitrary places had to be bent around that restriction.  The hardware of the day had no double-precision arithmetic, which much scientific work depends on.  Pulling results back off the card was slow and clumsy.  And every problem, however far from graphics, still had to be forced into the vocabulary of textures and pixels.  None of these were limits of the silicon’s raw ability to compute.  They were limits of the costume – the graphics interface the computation was made to wear.

That is what the hack era proved, and it is why it mattered.  It showed two things at once: that there was real demand for general-purpose computing on the GPU, and that the chip was genuinely capable of it.  The only thing in the way was the graphics disguise – and a disguise can be taken off.  The obvious next move was to build a GPU that presented itself as a general parallel processor from the start, paired with a language that addressed it directly, with no textures or rectangles in sight.  Ian Buck would carry the stream-and-kernel idea out of Brook and into the one company with the means to build exactly that.  The company was NVIDIA, the result would be called CUDA, and putting it into silicon meant spending transistors on a bet that almost no one outside the company yet understood.
