Section 1 – The pixel problem Top ↑

A 1080p frame is two million pixels.  At 60 frames per second, that is 120 million color decisions per second.  The triangle rasterization method makes each of those decisions independent of every other – and that independence is what made the problem solvable with fixed-function hardware: silicon that could do exactly one thing – rasterize triangles – and nothing else.

The hundred-thousand-dollar workstation

Before GPUs existed, real-time 3D graphics belonged to Silicon Graphics.  SGI sold workstations that cost more than houses – $100,000 and up – to Hollywood studios, the military, and aerospace companies.  Jurassic Park's dinosaurs, flight simulators for the Air Force, crash simulations for automakers – that was where real-time 3D rendering lived.  The entire market was professional, the budgets were enormous, and the idea that a $200 consumer card would eventually obliterate this business model was, in 1992, absurd.

SGI's hardware used custom graphics pipelines built from specialized chips.  The RealityEngine (1993) could render 500,000 lit, textured, antialiased polygons per second – a staggering number at the time, achieved with dedicated rasterization hardware that did one job and did it fast.  This was not general-purpose computing.  These chips could not run a spreadsheet.  They were hardwired to transform vertices, clip polygons to the screen boundary, interpolate colors across triangle surfaces, and write pixel values to a frame buffer.  Every step was fixed in silicon.

The fixed-function approach worked because the graphics pipeline is inherently sequential in structure but massively parallel in data.  Every triangle passes through the same stages – vertex transformation, clipping, rasterization, texturing, depth test – but millions of triangles can be in flight simultaneously, each independent of the others.  If you know exactly what operations will be performed, you can build a circuit that performs them without the overhead of instruction fetch, decode, and scheduling that a general-purpose processor requires.  You trade flexibility for throughput.  The pendulum, at this moment, is fully on the special-purpose side.

Jim Clark, SGI's founder, left the company in 1994 to start Netscape.  Even the man who built the graphics workstation industry thought the web was a bigger bet than graphics hardware.  He was right about the web.  He was wrong about graphics – but the future of graphics would not belong to SGI.  It would belong to the consumer market that SGI never took seriously.

Why triangles

Every surface you have ever seen rendered on a computer – every game character, every CAD model, every animated film – was built from triangles. Not squares, not arbitrary polygons, not curves.  Triangles.  This is not an accident of history or a limitation of early hardware.  It is a mathematical necessity that was recognized long before computers existed – and then put to work, with consequences nobody anticipated, by an engineer at a Boeing facility in Seattle.

In 1980, Loren Carpenter was working in Boeing's computer-aided design group, trying to render realistic terrain around airplane models.  He wanted mountains.  The standard approach – hand-modeling each peak and valley with control points – was too slow and produced landscapes that looked artificial.  Then, browsing a bookshop on University Avenue in Seattle, he found Benoît Mandelbrot's Fractals: Form, Chance and Dimension – and read it cover to cover, twice.  Fractals.  Self-similar geometry at every scale.

“I knew at once that I could apply that math to my problem,” Carpenter later recalled.  His technique was midpoint subdivision: take a triangle, displace the midpoints of its edges by a random amount, and split it into four smaller triangles.  Repeat.  At each level of recursion, the surface gains detail – ridges, valleys, the jagged fractal structure of real terrain.  The mathematics of this decomposition – breaking surfaces into simplices, the simplest possible polygonal element – had been developed by Henri Poincaré in the 1890s for studying topology.  Poincaré was not thinking about rendering.  He was classifying surfaces.  But the tool he built turned out to be exactly what Carpenter needed, eight decades later.

On July 14 of that year, Carpenter presented Vol Libre at SIGGRAPH – a two-minute film of a camera soaring over fractally generated mountains, banking and diving like a hang glider, finally coming to rest on a tiny Utah teapot sitting on a mountainside.  The audience of a thousand engineers gave him a standing ovation.  Ed Catmull and Alvy Ray Smith – already legends in the field, running the computer graphics division at Lucasfilm – walked up afterward and asked if he could start in October.  A Boeing CAD engineer, hired on the spot, because he saw what a triangle could do.

Carpenter died on December 21, 2025, at the age of 78.  His technique – listed by IEEE Spectrum as “the midpoint subdivision method for rendering fractals” – became the Genesis effect in Star Trek II: The Wrath of Khan, the first fully computer-generated sequence in a feature film.  He went on to co-invent the Reyes rendering architecture and co-author PhotoRealistic RenderMan at Pixar.  But the essential insight happened in a bookshop, recognizing century-old mathematics when he saw it.

The rasterization bargain

The reason triangles won – and the reason GPU hardware is built around them to this day – is a bargain between mathematics and silicon.  A triangle is the 2D simplex: the minimum polygon.  Three vertices define a plane, always, with no ambiguity.  A quad can be non-planar.  A pentagon can be concave.  A triangle cannot.  This guarantee means you can build a circuit that answers “is this pixel inside this triangle?” with three edge-function evaluations – a handful of multiplies and compares per pixel – and that circuit will never encounter a degenerate case.

In 1988, Juan Pineda published “A Parallel Algorithm for Polygon Rasterization” at SIGGRAPH, formalizing the edge-function approach that modern GPU rasterizers still use.  The algorithm is inherently parallel: every pixel can be tested independently.  You can evaluate thousands of edge functions simultaneously if you have enough hardware, and the result is always correct because the triangle’s mathematical properties guarantee it.  Gary Tarolli, the CTO of 3dfx, designed the Voodoo rasterizer using Pineda-style setup and rasterization.  Every GPU since has used some descendant of the same idea.

This is the rasterization bargain: you accept that every surface in the world will be approximated as a mesh of flat triangles, and in return you get hardware that can process those triangles at extraordinary speed because the per-triangle computation is small, uniform, and embarrassingly parallel.  The approximation is lossy – a sphere is not really made of flat facets – but if you use enough triangles, the human eye cannot tell.  And the number of triangles you can afford is exactly the question that drives GPU hardware scaling.

The fixed-function graphics pipeline of the 1990s was the purest expression of this bargain.  Every transistor on the chip was dedicated to one task: turning a stream of triangles into a frame of pixels, as fast as possible.  The pipeline could not do anything else.  It could not sort a list, multiply matrices for reasons unrelated to graphics, or run a neural network.  It was a machine for rasterizing triangles, and that is all it was.

That was about to change – not because anyone wanted general-purpose computing on a graphics chip, but because game developers wanted prettier water.
